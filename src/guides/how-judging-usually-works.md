Judging can feel like a black box. It is less mysterious than it looks: most events follow a small number of patterns, and the rules usually say which one applies. Knowing how it works helps you build and present for the people who decide.

## Who judges

Judges are chosen by the organisers. They are often a mix of engineers from sponsor companies, people from the event's theme area (for example, clinicians at a health hackathon), past winners and the organisers themselves.

The platform listing an event is usually not the judge. Devpost, for example, says that the vast majority of hackathons on its site are run independently by their organisers, who set the rules, review submissions and choose winners. A smaller number of events are managed with Devpost's support. Either way, the event's own rules are the reference.

## What judges score

Most events publish judging criteria on the event page or in the official rules. When we read several hundred Devpost event pages, the criteria named most often were:

- presentation,
- technical execution or technical implementation,
- impact or potential impact,
- innovation and creativity,
- design and user experience,
- functionality.

On Devfolio, every listing we read named the same five judging factors: technicality, originality, practicality, aesthetics and "wow factor".

Each criterion is usually scored on a simple scale, and scores are combined across judges. Weighting varies. If the rules give weights, plan your time around them.

## Common judging formats

**Expo or science fair.** Teams stay at a table and judges visit in turn, often for a few minutes each. Several rounds may run, with finalists chosen for a later round.

**Stage presentations.** Teams present one after another with a fixed time slot. Sometimes this is used only for finalists.

**Online review.** Judges review the submission page: the description, screenshots, links and demo video. This is the norm for online events and common for the first round of large in-person events.

**Sponsor and track prizes.** Sponsors often judge their own prizes separately, looking for good use of their product or API. Some platforms let you opt into specific prizes when you submit; others ask you to mention the tools you used. Devfolio's guide, for example, suggests naming sponsor tools in the "Technologies used" section so judges can find your project.

**Public or people's choice voting.** Some events add an audience vote, usually as one extra prize rather than the main result. It is less common than criteria-based judging: only a handful of the Devpost pages we read mentioned public voting.

## Eligibility checks

Before or during judging, organisers often check that submissions follow the rules: the project was built during the event, required parts such as the video are present, and the team is eligible. On Devpost, some hackathons (marked with a review icon on the overview page) check baseline eligibility for submissions made at least a week before the deadline, which gives teams time to fix problems. For short events, the safest approach is simply to read the requirements before you start building.

## How long results take

Timelines vary widely. In-person events often announce winners at a closing ceremony the same day. Online events can take days or weeks while judges review every submission. Organisers are responsible for announcing results within the timeframe they state in the rules, so check there first.

On hackathon.so, ended event pages show winners when they are published on Devpost or Devfolio, copied exactly as stated there. If nothing has been published yet, the page says so and shows when we last checked.

## What this means for your team

- **Read the criteria before you build.** It is easier to aim at a target you can see.
- **Make the project easy to evaluate.** Clear first paragraph, working links, a short video, screenshots.
- **Meet every requirement.** A missing video or a private repository can rule out a strong project.
- **Name your sponsor tools.** If you are aiming for a sponsor prize, make the connection obvious.
- **Be honest about what is mocked.** Judges see many demos and value candour.
