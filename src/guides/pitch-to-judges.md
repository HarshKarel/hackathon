A pitch is the frame around your demo. Its job is to make the problem matter, make the demo easy to follow and leave judges with a clear reason to score you well. It does not need to be polished like a startup pitch. It needs to be clear, honest and on time.

## Know your format and time limit

Find out early how judging works at your event. Common formats include:

- **Stage presentations** with a fixed time slot, sometimes followed by questions.
- **Expo or science-fair rounds**, where judges walk between tables and you repeat a short pitch many times.
- **Online judging**, where your video and written submission are the pitch.

Time slots are often short. Treat the limit as fixed: many events stop presenters when time runs out.

## A structure that works

For a short slot, divide the time roughly like this:

| Part | Share of time | What to cover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | About 15% | Who has the problem and why it hurts, in one or two sentences |
| Demo | About 50% | The core path, live or recorded |
| How it works | About 15% | The one or two technical decisions worth noticing |
| What is next | About 10% | What you would build next and who would use it |
| Close | About 10% | One sentence that sums up the project |

Start with the problem statement you wrote at the beginning of the event. If you skipped that step, read [how to pick a problem statement](/guides/pick-a-problem-statement/).

## Speak to the judging criteria

Judges usually score against criteria the event publishes. Across hundreds of Devpost event pages we read, the most common were presentation, technical execution, impact, innovation, creativity and design. Every Devfolio listing we read named the same five judging factors: technicality, originality, practicality, aesthetics and "wow factor". Your event's own list is what counts, so read it.

Make each criterion easy to score:

- **Impact:** name the user and the size of the problem.
- **Technical execution:** point out what is genuinely hard and working.
- **Innovation or originality:** say clearly what is new compared with existing tools.
- **Design or experience:** let the demo show it rather than describing it.
- **Presentation:** finish on time with a clear story.

## Write it, then cut it

Write the pitch out in full once, read it aloud, and time it. Then cut until it fits with room to spare. Remove:

- background that does not help a judge score you,
- lists of every technology you used (put those in the submission),
- anything you would need to apologise for.

Short sentences are easier to say under pressure and easier to follow.

## Rehearse the handoffs

If more than one person presents, decide who says what and practise the transitions. The person driving the demo should not also be explaining every click; split the roles. Agree a signal for "move on" if a section runs long.

## Prepare for questions

Judges often ask about:

- what is real and what is mocked,
- how it would work with real users or real data,
- what you would do with more time,
- who on the team built what,
- how it differs from an existing product.

Answer directly and briefly. "We did not get to that; here is how we would approach it" is a good answer. Guessing or overstating is not.

## Online and video pitches

When judging is online, your video and written submission are the whole pitch. The same structure applies: problem, demo, how it works, what is next. Put the most important information in the first lines of the description, because judges reviewing many entries read the top first.

## On the day

- Arrive early, test the screen and audio.
- Have the demo open and the backup video ready.
- Speak to the judges, not the slides.
- Stop on time, even mid-sentence if you must, and thank them.

A clear five-minute pitch for a modest project often scores better than a rushed pitch for an impressive one. If you want to understand what happens after you leave the room, read [how judging usually works](/guides/how-judging-usually-works/).
